## What Is a Data Set?

[Prompt Wrap Start]  
This section introduces the idea of a data set as a collection of information.  
Prompt: If we collected everyone’s shoe sizes in class, what would we call that list of numbers?  
[Prompt Wrap End]

A **data set** is a collection of related information, often written as numbers.  
- Example: Test scores, shoe sizes, or daily temperatures.  
Data sets help us look for patterns, make comparisons, and draw conclusions.

## Measures of Center

[Prompt Wrap Start]  
This section explains mean, median, and mode.  
Prompt: What are three different ways you can describe the “middle” of a data set?  
[Prompt Wrap End]

- **Mean**: the average (add all numbers, divide by how many).  
- **Median**: the middle value when numbers are in order.  
- **Mode**: the value that appears most often.  

Each measure gives a different picture of the data.

## Measures of Spread

[Prompt Wrap Start]  
This section introduces range and variability.  
Prompt: How can you tell if a data set is tightly packed or spread out?  
[Prompt Wrap End]

- **Range**: difference between the largest and smallest values.  
- **Interquartile Range (IQR)**: spread of the middle 50% of data.  
- **Standard Deviation**: how far numbers typically are from the mean.  

Spread tells us how consistent or varied the data is.

## Outliers and Their Impact

[Prompt Wrap Start]  
This section shows how unusual values affect results.  
Prompt: What happens to the mean if one test score is much higher or lower than the rest?  
[Prompt Wrap End]

An **outlier** is a value very different from the others.  
- Example: In 10, 11, 12, and 50, the “50” is an outlier.  
Outliers can pull the mean up or down, but they don’t change the median as much. Recognizing outliers is key to fair analysis.

## Comparing Data Sets

[Prompt Wrap Start]  
This section explains how to compare two or more sets of data.  
Prompt: How could you compare test scores from two different classes?  
[Prompt Wrap End]

We compare data sets by looking at:  
- Differences in center (mean, median).  
- Differences in spread (range, IQR, standard deviation).  
- Shape of distribution (clustered, symmetric, skewed).  

This helps us make evidence-based conclusions.
